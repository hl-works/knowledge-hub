Content Machine: three publishing projects become a single system

Claude · HL · September 1, 2026 · Haute Fidélité
marketingagentstraten-coursHaute Fidélité

Context

Three publishing projects each lived on their own: the “attested-content machine” (a v2 spec dated July 30, a foundation in place — collections, login, indexing guardrail, entity page with structured data), the “outbound” direction of my Knowledge building block (“publish this idea” from the Brain to this site), and the “to publish” trigger of the mail-and-tasks building block. Three inputs, three governances, one same problem: publishing what was lived, without losing your voice, without ever publishing alone.

On September 1, rereading the three specs side by side in the Brain, the conclusion was unavoidable: it's a single system. One capture, N destinations. The name: Content Machine.

What was done

  • The pivot: the Content Machine becomes the pivot building block for publishing. Knowledge's “outbound” direction is attached to it (Knowledge keeps only the “inbound” direction: the Brain and its index); the project's old name is kept as an alias so no links break.
  • The principles, reaffirmed: the base unit is the attested observation (a few media files, a voice memo or a note, at a place, at a time, by an identified person); the post, the article, the printed page are only renderings of it; witnessed and documented are kept separate, in the database and in form; the machine never decides; provenance is the product.
  • Three inputs, one same log: field capture (Telegram, under ten seconds, offline, partial sends), repurposing of the magazine (PDFs and visuals of the current issues and the archives — as the publisher, I hold the rights, which lifts one reservation from the spec), and “publish this idea” from a Brain document marked publishable.
  • Routing by context: a single Telegram group, one topic per context (personal, professional under my own name, magazine). Posting in a topic states the context; inference from the content is only a suggestion. Each context has its default visibility and its own rules (location metadata stripped, no indexing of minors, LinkedIn as draft only, hard embargo on the magazine side).
  • The day's decisions: Telegram stays the cockpit (WhatsApp ruled out: 24-hour window, imposed templates, re-encoded media that breaks print, a third party in the loop); the social layer stays pluggable behind a single interface, with a self-hosted, replaceable publishing tool rather than services billed per account; measurement doesn't depend on the publishing tool (Growth reads the platforms' native stats); the Atlas of Asia (Atlas d'Asie) — our travel journal from this summer — is the handmade pilot of the journal the machine will have to know how to replicate; the magazine's site and the travel journals are headed for a dedicated domain, while this site and the Atlas stay where they are.
  • The build order: social → web → print. We validate the engine where a bad output costs nothing. One external deadline: the Haute Fidélité show, late September.

What was hard / unexpected

Separating context from destination. As long as I was thinking “one Instagram account = one project”, nothing fit together. Context is provenance (where, when, why); destination is where it gets published; one context deploys to several destinations. That sentence collapsed three specs into one.

Giving up tools already evaluated. Two multi-account publishing services had been shortlisted in July; re-evaluated with several Instagram, LinkedIn and YouTube accounts, their per-account cost became a penalty. Dropping them was easy because the social layer had been designed pluggable: the adapter changes, the machine doesn't move.

Not over-governing. The Atlas of Asia is live, with family photos; the temptation was to add the visibility locks planned for the machine. Decision, owned: validated as is — we don't complicate what works.

Stack

The HL OS foundation (headless CMS + web framework + PostgreSQL, media on object storage), a provider-independent model router with small / large / fallback tiers and logging of every call, Telegram with its topics, a self-hosted publishing adapter behind a single interface, server-side rendering and structured data for the web, character-count calibration for print.

What it illustrates

When everyone can generate content, the only thing that still sets you apart is having been there — and being able to prove it. A useful content machine in 2026 isn't a machine for producing more: it's logistics between what was observed and where it needs to land, with locks before every output and a human who decides with one button.
