What's AI,
and what's an “LLM”?

Generative AI and “LLMs” explained without jargon, to decide fast: what they do, who the big players are (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Mistral…), and above all what they can't do.

By Hugo Lahutte · May 31, 2026·Updated September 1, 2026 · ~6 min read

30-second take

  • Generative AI is a family of software that produces text, code or images from a simple instruction written in plain language.
  • The engine behind assistants like ChatGPT or Claude is called an LLM (large language model): a model trained on enormous amounts of text, whose only real talent is predicting the most plausible continuation of what you write to it — and that's enough to do stunning things.
  • The big players today: OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), Google (Gemini), Meta, Mistral (the French one), xAI (Grok), the Chinese open models (DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, Qwen), plus Microsoft (Copilot) and Perplexity. Versions change every month; the roles, much less.

1. Are AI and an LLM the same thing?

No — and that's the first confusion to clear up. People say “AI” about everything, but there are two very different things behind it.

“Classic” AI has existed for decades: it's the algorithms that recommend you a product, filter spam or optimize a delivery route. Useful, but specialized: each system does a single task, the one it was coded for.

The recent breakthrough is generative AI: a single system can write an email, summarize a 40-page PDF, translate, code, analyze a sales spreadsheet — without having been programmed for each of those tasks. That versatility is what changes the game. And the LLM is precisely the engine of that generative AI for text: a subset of the big word “AI,” not a synonym.

2. What's an LLM, concretely?

An LLM is a model that has “read” a gigantic share of what's written online, and has learned a single thing: guessing the next word. You give it a beginning, it predicts the most probable continuation, word after word.

Seen like that, it looks like your phone's autocomplete. The difference is scale: by ingesting so much text, the model ends up “understanding” enough of the structure of language to summarize, argue, translate or write code — capabilities that emerge without having been explicitly programmed.

The right mental model to keep: an LLM doesn't recite a database, it generates. It doesn't go “look up” an answer stored somewhere — it rebuilds it on the fly. That's its strength (it adapts to any request) and its weakness (it can rebuild it wrong, see below).

3. What are the best-known LLMs?

The market has settled around a few families. Versions follow one another fast (every few months) — what matters is knowing who does what:

  • OpenAI — ChatGPT: the best known to the general public, the one that launched the wave in late 2022. GPT-5.6 family since July 2026, and “ChatGPT Work,” an agent for professional tasks.
  • Anthropic — Claude: focused on reliability, reasoning and “serious” work (writing, code, analysis, agents). Claude 5 family since summer 2026 (Sonnet 5, Opus 5, Fable 5.1). It's the one I build everything on (see the dedicated guide).
  • Google — Gemini: integrated into the Google ecosystem (and into its AI overviews in search), very strong on long documents, video and multimodal; also publishes open models (Gemma).
  • Meta: long the champion of “open weights” (Llama, which you can host yourself); in 2026, low-cost agentic models (Muse Spark) and an emphasis on advertising and assistants inside its apps.
  • Mistral: the French/European champion, partly open — the sovereignty argument when hosting your data in Europe matters. A thousand employees in 2026, very good on specialized building blocks (document reading, robotics), a notch behind the frontier on general-purpose models.
  • The Chinese open models — DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi, Qwen: open, formidable on performance per dollar, and closing the gap with the leaders at every release (but raising confidentiality questions when you use their online services).
  • xAI — Grok: Elon Musk's model, plugged live into X, which in 2026 became a serious option for agents thanks to its performance/price ratio (Grok 4.6).
  • Microsoft — Copilot: the AI built into Windows and the Office suite. Under the hood, mostly partner models (OpenAI, and Claude on its Azure cloud), plus its first in-house models.
  • Perplexity: less a model than an answer engine — it searches the web and answers with its sources cited; and since 2026 a “Computer,” an agent that executes tasks by orchestrating other people's models.

4. What can an LLM NOT do?

This is the part people skip too often, and it's the one that avoids nasty surprises.

  • It's not a search engine or a database. Because it generates instead of retrieving, an LLM can make up a wrong answer with total confidence — that's called a hallucination. On a number, a product reference, a date: you check.
  • By default, it's connected neither to the internet in real time nor to your data. For it to read your email, your Shopify or your ERP, you have to explicitly connect it (that's the whole point of another guide).
  • It's a copilot, not an oracle. Used well, it saves a crazy amount of time; used with blind trust, it does damage. The whole method is right there.
