Odoo or a custom ERP: which one to choose in 2026?

The question keeps coming back: pick an off-the-shelf ERP like Odoo, or build your own now that AI makes the cost of development collapse? I practice both in parallel — Odoo extended daily at Cobra, and a homemade ERP built from scratch in 5 days. Here's the verdict, criterion by criterion, with no religion.

By Hugo Lahutte· June 16, 2026·Updated September 1, 2026·~7 min read
comparatif erp odoo strat

The 30-second verdict

  • Pick Odoo (or an off-the-shelf ERP) when the business is standard and regulated — accounting, payroll, inventory, purchasing. A proven foundation covers 80% of the need from day one, and you don't rewrite an accounting system.
  • Pick custom-built when the business is specific, when no software models it without forcing you to “fit into boxes,” or when you want your UX and your brand identity. That's where AI changes everything.
  • The trap to avoid: reasoning on the license price instead of the total cost (integration, customization, maintenance, dependency). And falling into “everything custom” for generic functions the market already does very well.

1. The match in one table

The decision isn't about “which one is better” in the absolute, but about the weight you give to each criterion. Here are the eight that really matter.

Odoo vs. custom ERP — criterion-by-criterion comparison (2026).
Criterion Odoo (buy / extend) Custom ERP (build with AI)
Upfront cost Per-user/month license (Enterprise) + integration days. Community is free but limited. Almost nothing in licenses. The cost = your framing time + the AI subscription. Development collapses.
Total cost over 3 years (TCO) Grows with the number of users and the customization. Maintenance of custom modules to plan for. Hosting + managed database = a few dozen €/month, stable whatever the number of users.
Time to launch Fast on the standard: existing modules run right away (accounting, CRM, inventory). Everything has to be built — but much faster than before: a working core in days, not months.
Business flexibility Good within Odoo's framework; beyond it, you have to develop modules and live with the imposed data model. Total. The software fits your exact business, not the other way around. No box you're forced into.
Maintenance & updates Core maintained by the vendor, but every version upgrade can break your custom modules. 100% on you — but a smaller codebase, in your hands, and AI speeds up fixes too.
Dependency Vendor (roadmap, pricing, terms) + often a partner integrator. You / your team. No license ransom, but the knowledge has to stay documented and shared.
Compliance (accounting, payroll) Covered and kept up to date by the vendor (standards, tax rules). A huge advantage on the regulatory side. Not to be reinvented. The right reflex: let an external source of truth handle the regulatory part.
Risk / reversibility Low technically (proven product), but a costly exit once locked into the ecosystem. Quality risk if nobody verifies; but you own your code and your data, so it's reversible.

2. Odoo in detail

Odoo is a very complete modular management suite (CRM, sales, purchasing, inventory, accounting, HR…), available as an open-source Community edition and a paid Enterprise edition. It's an excellent default for the vast majority of SMBs: the standard is solid, the community huge, and the essentials of a classic business are covered without writing a line of code.

Where Odoo wins: a quick start on generic functions, accounting and tax compliance maintained for you, an ecosystem of modules and integrators, and a “clean” exit as long as you stay within the framework. At Cobra, that's exactly my choice: an Odoo I extend almost every day with Claude Code, rather than rewriting everything.

Where it gets stuck: as soon as your business steps outside the framework, you develop modules, you live with Odoo's data model, and every version upgrade becomes a regression test of your customizations. Community's “free” quickly melts into integration and maintenance costs.

3. The custom ERP in detail

Building your own is no longer the six-figure, 18-month project of yesteryear. With AI at the keyboard and a human at the helm, a working core gets built in days. The point isn't to “do Odoo, but worse”: it's to model exactly your business, with your UX and your brand identity.

Where custom-built wins: total flexibility, a near-fixed operating cost (no per-user license), ownership of the code and the data, and an experience tailored to your team — not a generic compromise. Decisive for a business the market doesn't model.

Where it gets stuck: everything rests on your ability to decide and verify. No vendor safety net: quality, backups, security, regulatory compliance are your responsibility. And above all, don't rewrite what's generic and critical (accounting) just for the pleasure of custom-built.

4. Who should choose what

Rather Odoo

Standard business, a team to equip fast

  • Accounting, payroll, inventory, purchasing at the heart of the need
  • Strong regulatory compliance requirements
  • Non-technical team, no in-house AI pilot
  • Growth through modules that already exist
·
Rather custom-built

Specific business, UX requirements

  • Processes the market doesn't model (or models badly)
  • You want your interface and your brand identity, not a vendor's
  • A business owner / a team that knows how to steer AI
  • A need for very specific dashboards and automations

And most of the time, the real answer is hybrid: an off-the-shelf foundation for the generic and the regulatory, custom pieces grafted alongside for what sets you apart. That's precisely what I do in practice.

My field experience

I didn't pick a side: I live both paths in parallel, which makes the comparison concrete.

Custom-built — I built HF-OS, the internal ERP of my magazine Haute Fidélité (CRM, ad sales, invoicing, payment reminders), in 5 days, 96 pull requests, ~20,000 lines and ~16 hours of effective work, alone, steering Claude Code. Operating cost: a few dozen euros a month. The full story and the numbers: Should you still buy an ERP?

Extending the market — at Cobra, I start from an existing Odoo that I modify and complete almost daily, with homemade dashboards on the side for what Odoo does badly. See the sessions product creation agent and Odoo catalog integration.

My conclusion from the field: the needle has moved. Custom-built becomes rational much earlier than before — but extending a standard remains the right reflex for everything generic and critical.

6. Frequently asked questions

Odoo or a custom ERP: which one to choose?

Odoo (or any off-the-shelf ERP) when the business is standard and regulated — accounting, payroll, inventory, purchasing: you don't rewrite an accounting system, and a proven foundation covers 80% of the need right away. A custom ERP when the business is specific, when no software models it without contortions, or when you want your own UX and brand identity. Now that AI makes the cost of customization collapse, custom-built becomes rational much earlier than before.

How much does a custom ERP built with AI cost?

The line item that collapses is development: HF-OS, a working publishing ERP (CRM, ad sales, invoicing, payment reminders), was built in ~16 hours of effective work over 5 days and 96 pull requests, by one person steering Claude Code. The operating cost comes to a few dozen euros a month (hosting + managed database + backups) plus the AI subscription. Compare that with per-user licenses and the integration days of an off-the-shelf ERP. The real remaining cost of custom-built isn't money: it's the time spent framing and verifying.

Is Odoo really free?

Partly. Odoo Community is open source and free when self-hosted, but limited (no full accounting and some apps missing). The Enterprise edition is paid, billed per user per month, and the real bill mostly comes from integration, customization and module maintenance. So “free” doesn't mean “no cost”: it's the total cost of ownership you have to look at, not the license price.

Do you need to know how to code to build your ERP with AI?

Not to write the code line by line — the AI takes care of that. But you need to know how to decide and verify: frame the scope, make the architecture calls, set the guardrails (backups, access, personal data), review. It's steering work, not typing. A business owner who knows their trade and learns to steer AI can produce the capacity of a small team; someone who doesn't want to verify anything shouldn't risk it on a critical tool.
