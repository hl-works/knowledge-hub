How to talk to AI properly in 2026: from prompt to brief

A good prompt makes a good answer. A good brief makes good work — when the AI chains steps together on its own, what you write beforehand matters more than ever. The recipe that still holds, what's changed, and what you should stop retyping.

By Hugo Lahutte · May 31, 2026 · Updated September 2, 2026 · ~7 min read

In 30 seconds

  • The basic recipe hasn't moved: context + task + format + constraints, and an example of the result you want.
  • What's changed: the AI no longer just answers, it works (reads your files, acts in your tools, chains steps together). The prompt becomes a brief: a goal, a success criterion, checkpoints.
  • The real lever is no longer the magic phrase: it's what the AI knows before your request. An instruction you keep retyping is a rule in disguise; a procedure you keep re-explaining is a skill in disguise.

1. The basic recipe (still true)

A prompt is the instruction you write to the AI. Nothing technical, but it's what changes an answer the most: the same AI returns generic mush or a clean deliverable depending on how you ask. No secret formula — clarity. Four building blocks are enough for a simple request:

2. From prompt to brief: what's changed

In May, a request to the AI produced an answer. In September 2026, it often triggers a piece of work: Claude reads a folder, fixes twenty files, sends an email pending your approval, runs for an hour in the background. Claude Code's default mode chains actions without asking your permission at every step; Cowork does the same on your desktop. A one-line instruction that was enough for an answer becomes dangerous for a mission.

Hence the shift from prompt to brief. Two more building blocks, and a change of nature for the first one:

  • Context is provided, no longer described. Instead of explaining your business, you hand over the files, the folder, the connector. “Here's the sales spreadsheet” beats three paragraphs about your sales. What's permanent (tone, rules, tools) no longer even belongs in the prompt: it lives in a CLAUDE.md.
  • The goal and the success criterion. “We'll know it's done when…”: all 175 pages share a consistent tone, the script runs without errors, the email fits in 120 words. An AI acting on its own needs to know when to stop — otherwise it keeps going, or stops too early.
  • The checkpoints. For a long mission: “propose a plan before you start,” “show me the diff before pushing,” “one question at a time if something's ambiguous.” You keep control at the moments that matter, not on every line.

3. Vague prompt, precise prompt, brief

The same need, three levels. The second is enough for an answer; the third is what I write when the AI has to deliver on its own.

And the brief, for a mission — a real one here, for an invoice check handed to Claude Code:

Goal: check that every invoice in the /factures-aout folder matches
an Odoo order. We'll know it's done when: a table lists each invoice
with the order number found, or “to check” with the reason.

Context: the invoices are PDFs; Odoo access is read-only.
Rules: change nothing in Odoo; never guess an amount.

Steps: 1) read the folder and propose a 5-line plan, wait for my OK;
2) process the invoices; 3) deliver the table + the list of doubtful cases.
If something is ambiguous, ask ONE question, then continue.

Notice what's missing: no explanation of what an invoice is, no instruction about tone, no naming rule. All of that is already in the permanent context. The brief only contains what's specific to this mission.

4. The reflexes of 2026

  • Give an example. Showing a model of the result is worth ten lines of explanation. For a recurring deliverable, the example ends up in a skill.
  • Ask for the plan before execution. On anything beyond ten minutes of work: “propose a plan, wait for my OK.” Claude Code has a dedicated mode for this; elsewhere, the sentence is enough. It's the “think before acting” principle of the four principles.
  • One question at a time. Allow the AI to ask you one question when something's ambiguous — and forbid the burst of ten. You avoid off-target answers without getting grilled like at the police station.
  • Iterate on the result, not on the sentence. “Too long,” “keep the second paragraph, cut the rest,” “show me what you changed.” Back-and-forth is normal; hunting for the perfect wording on the first try is not.
  • Break it down. A big mission into steps, with a checkpoint between each one. For a long project, a plan file the AI ticks off as it goes.
  • Say what must not be touched. “Only change this file,” “read-only on Odoo,” “don't push anything without showing me.” With an AI that acts, the negative constraint counts as much as the positive instruction.
  • Close cleanly. When a conversation gets long, ask for a handoff brief: where things stand, what's left, the decisions made. The next session picks up from there instead of re-explaining everything.

5. The three places an instruction lives

The question to ask about every instruction: am I going to write it again? If so, it's not in the right place.

In my setup, the casual tone (“tu” in French), “no preamble,” “one question at a time” or “flag it, don't change it on your own” are never typed anymore: they're in the CLAUDE.md. Publishing a journal entry (page, index, sitemap, feeds, translation) is no longer explained: it's a skill. This very guide was born from a two-line brief, because everything else was already known. That's the central idea of the guide What is “context”, and why it's everything: the prompt is 20%; the context is the rest.

6. Depending on the tool: Chat, Cowork, Code

  • In Claude Chat — the basic recipe is enough. For a recurring topic, create a project with its instructions and documents: the context is provided once and for all.
  • In Cowork — point to the folder or the connector (Gmail, Drive, Shopify…) rather than describing what it contains, and say explicitly what must be validated before being sent or changed. The guide AI proposes, the human validates spells out the rule.
  • In Claude Code — write a brief (goal, criterion, steps, checkpoints), ask for the plan, let it run, review the diff. For a task that comes back every day, it's no longer a prompt: it's a routine.

Choosing the tool itself is covered in Chat, Cowork, or Code.
