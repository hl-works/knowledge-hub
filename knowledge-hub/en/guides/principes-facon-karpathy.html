The 4 principles I apply with AI (Karpathy-style)

Think before acting, keep it simple, touch only what's strictly necessary, steer by the goal. Four reflexes drawn from the way Andrej Karpathy teaches working with models — and what they become when the AI no longer just writes text but acts on your behalf.

1. Who Karpathy is (and why it matters)

Hard to find anyone more credible on the subject. Karpathy co-founded OpenAI, ran AI at Tesla, and above all he's one of the best explainers of AI out there: his courses and videos trained a generation. He's the one who coined the phrase “vibe coding” and, later, the idea that an LLM works better with a wiki than with a pile of conversations. In May 2026, he joined Anthropic — when someone of that caliber picks a house, you listen.

Honesty first: these are not “Karpathy's four official rules.” It's my synthesis, inspired by the way he teaches working with agents. It opened my base instruction file from May to August; since then, Claude carries it in memory, and it opens every repo's CLAUDE.md, word for word:

3. What they become when the AI acts on its own

In May, these principles mostly governed the quality of an answer: fewer walls of text, less off-topic. Since then, Claude Code chains actions without asking permission at every step (auto mode, the default since August 14), Cowork acts inside desktop tools and in a browser, agents run overnight. The same principles change roles: they become the execution discipline of an AI that does things on your behalf.

A concrete example of each, from my own setup. The plan before execution: the redesign of this site started with an audit and a validated plan, not with modified files. The simplest tool: the Growth building block was scoped in a weekend because we built a queue of actions, not a platform. The surgical part: the production release of an Odoo module split into reversible deploys. The verifiable goal: this site has a health-check script that counts entries, checks the indexes and the feeds, and that a Claude runs before every publication.

4. The fifth reflex: compounding

It isn't Karpathy's, but it's what makes the other four hold up over time: a mistake becomes a rule, a session becomes a capture. My principle #3 isn't theoretical — it was born from a production release that broke everything because we had pushed too much at once. Since then, every recurring correction is retained — in memory, where Claude Code often notes it down itself, or in the repo's CLAUDE.md when it's a rule for the code — and every significant session goes into my Brain. That's the subject of the Compound Engineering guide.

5. From “vibe coding” to the “LLM wiki”

Two of Karpathy's ideas have made their way since spring. “Vibe coding” — describing your intent and letting the AI type the code — has become commonplace: the Growth building block was scoped and delivered in a weekend with no spec. What sets people apart now is no longer knowing how to ask, it's context engineering: organizing what the AI knows before you ask it anything.

And his “LLM wiki” pattern — a knowledge base made of text files that the AI reads and maintains, rather than a pile of conversations — was formalized in June 2026 by Google Cloud in an open standard, the Open Knowledge Format. It's the format of my Brain. The four principles haven't moved; they just have a lot more surface to apply to.
