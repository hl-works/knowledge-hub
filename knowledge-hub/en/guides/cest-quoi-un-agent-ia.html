What's an “AI agent”?

An AI agent is Claude handed a goal and the means to act on its own, in a loop, until the result. What it makes possible, the forms it takes in 2026 (subagents, routines, hosted agents), what runs on my side — and the guardrail without which it spirals out of control.

By Hugo Lahutte · May 31, 2026 · Updated September 1, 2026 · ~6 min read

30-second take

  • An assistant answers; an agent acts: it chains steps on its own toward a goal (search, decide, execute, check, start again).
  • Plugged into your tools, it runs tasks without you: news watch, invoices, supplier price lists, pricing, monitoring numbers. In 2026, it multiplies (subagents), schedules itself (routines) and lives in the cloud.
  • The trap: without guardrails (clear goal, capped budget, limited frequency, success criterion), an agent loops and spirals out of control. I learned that the hard way. And the rule that makes it all possible: AI proposes, the human validates.

1. Assistant or agent: the real difference

When you chat with Claude, it's an assistant: you ask, it answers, you take back control. An agent is the step above: you set it a goal, and it chains the steps on its own — it searches, decides, acts, checks its result, and starts again if needed, until it delivers.

The key ingredient is autonomy in a loop + access to tools to act (the connectors).

2. The forms of an agent in 2026

A year ago, “an agent” meant a script calling a model in a loop. Today, it takes four forms, from the closest to you to the most autonomous:

  • The agent in your session — Claude Code or Cowork chaining the steps of a task while you watch (or don't). Since the Claude 5 models, that can last for hours: it delivers a tested feature, not a function.
  • Subagents — a main agent delegating subtasks to copies of itself, in parallel, in the background, each with its own context. Translate twenty pages, audit ten files, check five sources. Details in What's a skill.
  • Routines — a scheduled agent (every morning, every Monday), run in the cloud without your machine. This summer's novelty. See Loops, routines and scheduled tasks.
  • Hosted agents — an agent living on a server (yours, or at Anthropic's), plugged into your tools through the API, running 24/7 with its own secrets and its own log. That's the form of my invoice agent and my news watches.

3. What runs on my side

Not a list of intentions — what's in production as of September 1, 2026:

  • Two daily news watches (worldwide hi-fi across 64 sources, AI news) that read, sort, write and publish every morning on Telegram and here. Repaired and hardened in August: model picked automatically, alert if a source dies for three days, cost capped.
  • Cobra's supplier invoice agent: it reads the inbox every fifteen minutes, extracts each invoice, attaches it to the right purchase order and pushes it for validation. It can only attach to an order that exists — that's what keeps it from drifting.
  • The app that reads supplier price lists: extracting references and prices, matching against the database, detecting duplicates, human validation before any write. No created product is published online automatically.
  • The price alignment engine: it recomputes target prices against the competition at each data delivery, never at a loss, in observation mode first and then live, family by family — going live remaining a human decision.
  • Dev request review: the AI asks questions, checks its hypotheses against the real database in read-only mode, and concludes — before a single line of code is written.
  • Growth: the action queue that brings together sales, acquisition, visibility, email and social, and proposes the next useful piece of work with its evidence. Automatic reads, validated writes.

What they have in common isn't the technology. It's that each one has a scope, a budget, and a clear line between what it does alone and what it submits.

4. The essential guardrail

An agent without a frame is a robot let loose. I had one that kept relaunching itself in a loop and burned its budget every day without producing anything usable. The lesson, now a rule on my side: every agent needs four things before it's launched —

  1. an explicit goal (“we'll know it's good when…”),
  2. a hard budget cap,
  3. a limited frequency (not a permanent loop),
  4. a measurable success criterion.

And a fifth, since agents started acting inside tools: automatic reads, validated writes. An agent reads, proposes, prepares; a person validates anything that changes a price, a stock level, a campaign, an email, a publication. That's not excessive caution: it's what lets me keep six agents running while I sleep. The details — three autonomy regimes, the flow of an action, the locks — are in AI proposes, the human validates.

One threat specific to agents, worth knowing: an agent that reads a web page or an email can run into booby-trapped instructions there (a prompt injection). The 2026 models resist it far better, but the real protection is still limited permissions: an agent that can't send money can't be manipulated into doing it.

5. Where to start

Small. A single agent, a single task, read-only first (it watches and alerts, it touches nothing), and you prove it's reliable before giving it a button — then a second agent. The “agents everywhere” big bang is the best way to break everything. My news watch ran for months before I let an agent write into Odoo.
