Content Machine:
one capture, N destinations

Anyone can generate content with AI — which is precisely why it's no longer worth anything. My content machine does the opposite: it doesn't produce content, it carries a presence. A photo, a voice memo, a place, a time, a person — and the machine proposes where and in what form it should land. Without ever deciding on its own.

1. The problem: generated content is no longer worth anything

In 2026, producing ten posts, a blog article and a newsletter takes five minutes. Everyone does it; the feeds are full of smooth texts that nobody actually lived. For a magazine, a brand or a retailer, that's not just useless: it's dangerous, because it dilutes the only thing that still sets you apart — having been there.

My problem was concrete. A print magazine (Haute Fidélité) and its archives, an editorial brand (HiFi Lovers), a family travel journal, this Knowledge Hub, a LinkedIn account, a trade show coming up. A huge amount of raw material, and every time the same friction: capturing is easy, formatting eats time, and nothing connects what was seen to what gets published.

2. The principle: carrying a presence

The Content Machine doesn't produce content. In it, AI is the logistics between what was observed in the field and the place where it needs to land. Four rules, non-negotiable:

  • The basic unit is the attested observation: a few photos, a voice memo or a note, at a place, at a time, by an identified person. The post, the article, the print page are only renderings of it.
  • Witnessed and documented are kept separate, in the database and in the form. What I saw doesn't have the same status as what I read, and the reader must be able to see it.
  • The machine never decides. It proposes a destination along with its reasoning; the human settles it with one button.
  • Provenance is the product. Who, where, when: that's the first-hand experience signal that search engines and answering AIs reward.

3. Three inputs, one shared log

4. Context ≠ destination

The distinction that unlocked everything. The context is the provenance: where, when, why. The destination is where it publishes. One context deploys to several destinations, and each has its own rules:

  • Personal — public and indexed: journal, personal Instagram, book. Its own rules: location metadata stripped, a delay before publishing a position, no indexing of minors.
  • Professional, in my own name — private, step by step: LinkedIn as draft only, never auto-published. The only place where judgment is legitimate — so nothing automatic.
  • Haute Fidélité — public, with a hard embargo: site, Instagram, newsletter, print. Judgment forbidden under the magazine's byline; facts and vintages checked.

Technically, it's a single Telegram group with one topic per context. Posting in a topic is enough; inferring from the content is only a suggestion. Capture stays under ten seconds, which is the only way to really capture.

5. The locks, before any publication

  1. Embargo as a hard block. A release date is a lock, not content: door closed until the date.
  2. Visibility lock. A media file showing a restricted person — minors included — is excluded from the social queues; only a manual override naming the person unlocks it.
  3. Witnessed / documented kept separate in the database and in the form.
  4. LinkedIn = draft only.
  5. Never a judgment under the magazine's byline.
  6. Publication on an explicit action, idempotent, with a readable error in Telegram. No cron that publishes on its own.

On the social networks, the engagement rule is just as strict: DMs and replies to comments are a reader relationship, not automated growth. No automatic likes, no automatic follows. Three regimes: mechanical = automatic; factual reply = generated then validated with one button; judgment = human draft.

6. Destinations and their life cycle

Not all destinations have the same lifespan, and that's what dictates the build order:

  • A bad output costs nothing

  • Magazine site, journal, this Knowledge Hub (evolving)
  • Newsletter (derived)
  • Print, book (permanent, dated, calibrated to a character count)

The social layer is deliberately pluggable: a replaceable publishing adapter behind a single interface. The site remains the source of truth, the social networks are satellites. Measurement, for its part, doesn't depend on the publishing provider: Growth reads the platforms' native statistics directly. You can switch publishing tools without losing the history.

7. The build order (and where it stands)

Social → web → print. You validate the engine where a bad output costs nothing, before trusting it with what's permanent. The stages: the irreversible foundation (domains, URLs, data schemas, media storage); timed Telegram capture; the documentation (entities, embeddings, model router); a first Instagram rendering to prove the adapter and the isolation of spaces; the duplicable journal — including the Atlas of Asia (Atlas d'Asie), our travel journal from this summer, as the hand-made pilot; the magazine site and LinkedIn; print; the book.

Dated snapshot · September 1, 2026

The v2 spec dates from July 30; the foundation (collections, sign-in, indexing guardrail, entity page with structured data) is in place; the September 1 pivot brought field capture, repurposing and “publish this idea” together into a single building block and settled the publishing tool (self-hosted, replaceable) rather than services billed per account. Next steps: capture in under ten seconds, first Instagram rendering. External deadline: the Haute Fidélité show, late September 2026. Still open: the dedicated name and domain for the magazine site and the journals.

8. What it changes for getting cited by AIs

GEO — getting cited in an AI's answer — rewards three things the machine produces by construction: one page per entity with its structured data (schema.org), explicit provenance (who, where, when — the first-hand experience signal), and a server-rendered site, readable without JavaScript, with an llms.txt. Mass-generated content has none of these three attributes. An attested observation has all of them.

In other words: the Content Machine isn't a machine for producing more. It's a machine for publishing only what was actually lived — and making it readable by humans and machines alike.
