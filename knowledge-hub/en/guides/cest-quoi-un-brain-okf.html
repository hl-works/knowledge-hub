What is a “Brain”,
and why it's my real asset

A Brain is the durable memory of everything you know and decide, written in text files that all your AIs can read. Mine is in the open OKF format, in a private repository. Here's what it is, how it fills up, and the rule that holds it together: a stale brain is worse than an empty brain.

By Hugo Lahutte · September 1, 2026 · ~7 min read

In 30 seconds

  • A Brain is a folder of Markdown files (plain text + a small header) that describes your organizations, your projects, your decisions and the way you work. Your AIs read it before answering you.
  • The OKF format (Open Knowledge Format, Google Cloud, June 2026) standardizes this “LLM wiki”: a single required field, links between files, an index. Zero software to install.
  • What holds it together isn't the tech: it's the update ritual, and two rules — a stale brain is worse than an empty brain; we never invent a page we don't know how to fill in.

1. The problem: AI has a short memory, and a siloed one

An AI assistant only “retains” what fits in its context window — the current conversation, plus whatever you've given it to read. Close the conversation, and everything starts from zero. The assistants' memory features help with your preferences, but they're tied to one tool, opaque, and impossible to re-read or correct by hand.

In my case, the symptom was clear: what I knew about Cobra, Haute Fidélité and the building blocks under construction lived in Claude project documents (not shareable between tools), in conversation threads, and in my head. Two Claude instances, a personal account and a work account, that didn't know the same things. And with every new piece of work, ten minutes of re-explaining.

2. What it is, concretely

A Brain is a private git repository full of Markdown files. Each file starts with a header (YAML: a few key: value lines) followed by a free-text body. Mine fits in seven folders:

The format is called OKF — Open Knowledge Format. It's an open specification published by Google Cloud in June 2026 that formalizes the “LLM wiki” popularized by Andrej Karpathy: Markdown, a YAML header with a single required field (type) and a few optional fields (title, description, resource, tags, date), Markdown links between documents. An OKF bundle can be read in any editor, renders on GitHub, and any search tool knows how to index it.

I kept six fields, plus two of my own: confidentialite (prive or publiable — private or publishable) — the guardrail made machine-readable — and auteur (me, Claude HL or Claude Cobra), because without it, there's no way six months later to judge how reliable a page on a given topic is. A small checking script (a linter) verifies all of this before every commit: fields present, links that exist, no secrets, note-specific rules.

# At the top of every file — YAML, then the text --- type: Brique # Entité | Brique | Projet | Perso | Méthode | Note | Session title: Knowledge description: One sentence. The one that makes you want to open it. tags: [knowledge, okf] timestamp: 2026-08-21T23:34:30Z confidentialite: prive # prive | publiable auteur: claude-hl # hugo | claude-hl | claude-cobra ---

3. How my AIs read it

Here's a decision that saved me weeks of work: no database, no server, no semantic search. The repository is private on GitHub; my Claude clients have the GitHub connector plugged in; they read the Brain directly, starting from the index.md and following the links. That's exactly what OKF's “progressive disclosure” is meant to allow.

Honesty: it works by navigation, not by search. That's enough as long as the bundle stays within a few dozen documents. I wrote down the switch criterion rather than building the step: the day “find me what we said about order dedup” no longer surfaces the right document, that'll be the signal to build a search index (embeddings + vector database). Not before. Writing down the signal that will tell you it's time to build almost always beats building as a precaution.

4. How it fills up (and doesn't go stale)

This is the part that matters. A knowledge base runs at two speeds: the log grows on its own (writing an entry is rewarding and risk-free), and the reference pages go stale in silence (updating them means re-reading what you wrote and admitting it has moved). Nothing flags the gap. An agent querying the base sees a structured, dated, authoritative document — and answers with it.

Hence the conviction that structures everything: a stale brain is worse than an empty brain. A missing document triggers a search; a wrong document triggers a decision. And the better the rest of the bundle is kept, the more you trust it, the more a stale page costs.

The remedy is a ritual, not a tool. At the end of a session, a capture skill does four things, in this order:

  1. re-read the session, identify the entity and the topic;
  2. write the sessions/ entry in the OKF format;
  3. propose targeted changes to the reference pages affected — that's the point you're tempted to skip, so it sits at the top of the procedure;
  4. run the confidentiality filter, then propose the commit.

Three rules complete the ritual:

  • We never invent the content of a shell (empty placeholder page). A document tagged “to fill in” opens with a box that says so and asks questions instead of answering them. An empty doc that says it's empty is honest; a doc filled in by guesswork is a trap that will go off six months later, at the precise moment you trust it.
  • A conviction without a counter-argument isn't a conviction, it's a reflex. Every note carries a “what could take it down” section. If there's nothing to put there, we say so, rather than writing a hollow section.
  • A document we no longer believe is never deleted: it moves to abandoned status. Knowing why you were wrong is reusable; a hole in the base isn't.

5. Brain, CLAUDE.md, Knowledge Hub: who does what

Three things that look alike and shouldn't be confused:

CLAUDE.md

How to work with me

  • Tone, principles, hard rules
  • Rarely changes
  • Loaded at every session
  • Private, versioned with the code
+
Brain (OKF)

What we know

  • Entities, projects, decisions, method, notes
  • Changes all the time
  • Read on demand, by navigation
  • Private — a connector, not a sync

And this Knowledge Hub is the third thing: the curated version of the Brain, rewritten to be read by humans and cited by AIs. Nothing marked prive can end up here, and even for a publiable document, it takes a human action. Moving from one to the other is an editorial act, never a sync. A good Brain contains things that will never get out — that's what makes it useful.

6. Where to start

Small. A private repository, an index.md that lists the folders, and three to five pages: one per important organization in your professional life (the four questions), one method page with your working rules. Plug the GitHub connector into your assistant, and check the criterion that matters: from any conversation, does a question about a project surface the right document?

Then, the ritual: at the end of every session that produced something, a session entry and an update of the pages affected. Three to five dictated lines are enough; the rest formats itself. And remember the rule that argues even harder for pruning than for updating: a base of fifteen living pages beats a base of two hundred half-dead ones.
