Why AI really changes the way we work

Not just another fad. Why generative AI is a shift of the same order as electricity, what it concretely changes for a business owner or a retailer in 2026 — three steps, not one — and what it does not change.

By Hugo Lahutte · May 31, 2026 · Updated September 2, 2026 · ~8 min read

In 30 seconds

  • Generative AI isn't one more tool: it's a general-purpose technology, like electricity — a layer that slips in everywhere, into every line of work.
  • In 2026, it no longer just answers: it acts inside your tools and chains steps on its own. Work climbs three steps: do → steer → frame a system that proposes.
  • The cost of customization collapses: an ERP for a magazine in five days, an action queue in a weekend. What doesn't change: judgment, responsibility, customer relationships — and validation before any sensitive action.

1. A general-purpose technology

The right analogy isn't “a new piece of software.” It's electricity. The phrase “AI is the new electricity” is Andrew Ng's (2017), and it hits the mark: electricity didn't improve one industry, it transformed all of them, because it plugged in everywhere.

Generative AI has that same horizontal character: the same tool writes an email, analyzes a sales spreadsheet, writes code, translates a contract — and, since this year, sorts the inbox, reads invoices, and aligns prices. It's not one profession that changes, it's every profession that gains a new layer.

2. The signals that this isn't hype

September 2026 benchmarks — names and amounts date quickly; it's the logic that matters, not the details.

The “revolution” talk is easy. What doesn't lie is where the people and the money of those with the most to lose are going:

  • Code writes itself, at scale. Anthropic reports that more than 80% of the code merged into its own codebase is now written by Claude; at Cursor, more than half of merged changes come from agents in the cloud. This is no longer a demo: it's the production output of the people who build the tools.
  • The best are switching houses for it. Andrej Karpathy joined Anthropic; CTOs of companies valued in the billions agreed to go back to being researchers. Jeff Bezos came out of semi-retirement to co-found an AI company.
  • Infrastructure is absorbing unprecedented sums. A quarter of nearly $100 billion in revenue for the leading chipmaker, hundreds of billions in commitments. The bottleneck is no longer the model, it's what runs it.
  • It has become a matter of state. The labs are preparing to go public; a model was suspended for three weeks by a government on national-security grounds. You don't suspend a fad.

The reading rule: when those with the most to lose reallocate their time and their capital to a subject, the subject is serious.

3. Three steps: do, steer, frame

In May, I described a shift: from “doing” to “steering and verifying.” Three months later, there's one more step, and it's the most important one.

  • Do → steer. You describe what you want and you verify, instead of doing everything by hand. A non-technical person does technical things: I've shipped management modules and advanced SEO without being a developer. The brake is no longer execution, it's knowing what to ask.
  • Steer → frame. When the AI acts inside your tools — reads the emails, prepares the product pages, proposes a price — you're no longer checking a function, you're framing a system: what it knows (the context), what it's allowed to do (the permissions), when it has to stop and ask you (the validation). That's what I built with HL OS: an action queue that says what to handle, with the evidence, and a button to validate.

4. The cost of customization collapses

This is the least visible and the deepest change for a small business. Custom software used to cost months and an agency; it now costs a few days and a good specification. Two examples at my place: HF-OS, the ERP of the Haute Fidélité magazine, built from scratch in five days with Claude Code; Growth, Cobra's action queue, framed over a weekend and in production on Tuesday — with ChatGPT and Codex, this time. The question “should you still buy software?” is no longer rhetorical: it comes up for every management layer.

The flip side: what costs now is knowing what you want and keeping it up to date. The specification, the context, the rules. Code has become the easy part.

6. What it does NOT change (and the pitfalls)

  • It doesn't replace judgment. AI proposes, you decide. It can be confidently wrong (see the guide on LLMs) — and an AI that acts gets things wrong faster than an AI that answers.
  • Responsibility can't be delegated. A price changed, an email sent, an order placed: that's you. Hence the rule that holds everything together at my place: automatic reads, validated writes.
  • The customer relationship stays human. The AI prepares the answer; it doesn't replace the contact, the goodwill gesture, the advice on the store floor.
  • The new trap: context debt. An AI framed with stale rules decides confidently on false premises. The frame has to be kept up to date like inventory — that's the subject of Compound Engineering.
  • The advantage goes to those who start early. Not in theory: by practicing, on real cases, with one specific chore to start.
