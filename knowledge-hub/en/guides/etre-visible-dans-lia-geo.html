Being visible in AI: GEO explained

GEO is SEO for the AI era: getting cited in the answers of ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude or Google's AI Overviews. Why it's becoming vital, what three months of practice on this site have taught — including one rule that changes everything: what isn't in the HTML doesn't exist for an AI.

In 30 seconds

  • SEO = being found on Google (the list of links). GEO = being cited in the answer written by an AI. More and more people ask their question to an AI: if it doesn't cite you, you don't exist in that answer.
  • The reflexes: pages in questions/answers, quotable statements, structured data, an identifiable entity, an llms.txt — and above all static HTML, because AI crawlers don't run JavaScript.
  • What weighs the most in 2026: provenance. First-hand experience, dated, signed, beats mass-generated content — for engines as much as for readers.

1. SEO, GEO: what's the difference

SEO (search engine optimization) tries to get you into a search engine's list of links — the goal is the click to your site. GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) aims at something else: being the source the AI cites when it writes its answer. Not a click in a list — a mention in the generated text, with or without a link.

2. Why it's becoming vital

Behavior is changing fast: many people ask their question directly to an AI instead of opening Google and comparing ten links — and Google itself answers with a generated overview above its list. September 2026 benchmarks: those AI Overviews are already weighing on the traffic of reference sites; Shopify has added an “AI visibility” section to its stores and opened purchasing to agents; tools measure whether your brand is cited in the answers of ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google, and which competitors are cited instead of you.

The consequence is brutal: you can rank very well on Google and be totally absent from an AI's answer on the same topic. GEO is making sure you also exist in that channel — the one eating a growing share of searches, and soon of purchases.

4. The rule that changes everything: what isn't in the HTML doesn't exist

The most useful lesson of my three months of practice, learned the hard way. This site's AI Watch — my freshest content, updated every day — was displayed by JavaScript. But the assistants' crawlers (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot) don't run JavaScript: they read the raw HTML, and nothing else. To them, my AI Watch was an empty page.

Since June, a script injects the last ten days into the page as static HTML on every publish, two RSS feeds exist, and the llms-full.txt file is regenerated automatically. The test to run on your own site: open a page with JavaScript disabled. What you see is what an AI sees. If your catalog, your reviews or your prices disappear, they don't exist for it.

5. Provenance is the product

The web is filling up with mass-generated text, and engines and AIs alike are learning to ignore it. What they value is the signal of first-hand experience: who saw what, where, when. A listening test actually done, a photo actually taken, a number measured in your own store. That's the principle of my Content Machine: one page per entity, structured data, an attested observation behind every output — and the exact opposite of content churned out by the yard. A useful paradox: AI helps you publish fast, but what it cites is what only a human could have witnessed.

6. Measuring (honestly)

GEO is still an emerging field: nobody sells a serious guarantee of being cited, and I don't claim to measure precisely how many AI answers cite this site. What I can measure, and what I advise you to measure:

  • Machine readability: your page without JavaScript, your structured data validated, your llms.txt accessible. It's binary, and it's the prerequisite.
  • Citations: the tools that ask ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google about your topics and note who gets cited — you, or your competitors. Asking ten customer questions to three assistants once a month already makes a decent dashboard.
  • Traffic coming from AIs: visits referred by assistants show up in your analytics; few, but highly qualified.

At Cobra, this tracking has become an item in Growth's action queue: the question “are we cited?” is handled like one more line, with its evidence, not like a belief.

7. This site as a textbook case

I'm not theorizing in a vacuum: this Knowledge Hub is my GEO testing ground. One page per question, subheadings phrased as questions, a summary at the top of every guide, JSON-LD (TechArticle, FAQPage, Person) on every page, visible update dates, an llms.txt and an llms-full.txt regenerated on every publish, two RSS feeds, and a mirrored English version. That's precisely why it's built in static HTML, clean and light: machine readability is a feature, not a detail.
