AI in practice, for a retailer or a small business

What a retailer or a small business can really do with AI in 2026, without a tech team: the use cases that pay off, the three tooling steps (write, act under validation, automate), what runs in my store, and where to start.

By Hugo Lahutte · May 31, 2026 · Updated September 2, 2026 · ~8 min read

In 30 seconds

  • No need to be a tech company: AI goes after the chores first — product pages, emails, supplier price lists, invoices, competitor prices, reporting.
  • Three tooling steps in 2026: write (Claude Chat), act under validation inside your tools (Cowork + Gmail, Drive, Shopify connectors), automate a recurring chore (an agent, a routine).
  • You start with one specific, recurring chore, read-only first, and you measure. The “big bang” almost always fails.

1. The use cases that pay off quickly

Seen from my store, the first wins aren't spectacular — they're recurring. What pays off is the chore that comes back every week:

  • writing and standardizing product pages in bulk, and the SEO that goes with them;
  • sorting emails and preparing replies;
  • reading supplier price lists (PDF, email) and preparing product creations and updates;
  • matching supplier invoices to purchase orders;
  • tracking competitor prices and proposing alignments;
  • cross-referencing sales between the online store and the back-office tool, and an automatic market watch.

2. Three tooling steps, without a tech team

I'm not a developer — my job is strategy, marketing, purchasing. The “you need to know how to code” barrier has largely fallen; what matters is knowing what to ask and how to verify. In 2026, it happens in three steps, and the first two require nothing more than a subscription:

  • Step 1 — write. Claude Chat, with one project per topic (tone, catalog, house rules). You paste a sales spreadsheet, it tells you what's going on; you give it ten product pages, it standardizes a hundred.
  • Step 2 — act under validation. Cowork works on your computer and inside your tools through connectors: Gmail, Drive, Shopify, the accounting software. It sorts, prepares replies, fills in product pages — and when a supplier portal has no connector, it opens its own browser and fills in the form on your behalf. Nothing goes out without a preview and a button. It's the step that has changed the most since the spring, and the one worth the most to a retailer.
  • Step 3 — automate. A chore that comes back every day deserves an agent or a routine: it runs without you, reports the result back to you, and asks you before writing anything.

3. What runs in my store

At Cobra, in production, without a tech team — built with Claude and, for the last building block, with ChatGPT and Codex:

  • Supplier price lists. An app reads the price lists (PDF or email), finds the matching products, detects duplicates, and prepares the creation or the update — human validation before any write. → An app that reads supplier price lists for me
  • Supplier invoices. An agent reads the emails, extracts each invoice, attaches it to the right purchase order, and pushes it into Teams for validation. → The case study
  • Prices against the competition. A rules engine recalculates target prices with every delivery of competitor prices, never at a loss, in observation mode first and then live, product family by product family. → Automatic alignment
  • Product pages. An agent prepares product creations from supplier data, to be validated in batches. → The product creation agent
  • AI Watch. A bot reads dozens of sources every day and publishes a summary on Telegram. → The AI Watch bot
  • Decisions. Rather than one more dashboard, an action queue that brings together sales, acquisition, visibility, email, and social, and says what to handle today, with the evidence. → Growth

4. Where to start (honestly)

  1. One specific, measurable, recurring chore. Not “AI everywhere”: product pages, or sorting the morning emails.
  2. Read-only first. The AI looks, proposes, prepares. It changes nothing until you've seen ten correct proposals in a row.
  3. Write down what it needs to know — your catalog, your tone, your rules — in a project or a file, once and for all. That's the context, and it's 80% of the result.
  4. Measure: the time saved on the chore, the mistakes avoided. Only then, expand.

5. What it costs

For the first two steps, a subscription of around twenty euros a month is enough; the higher tiers are for when you run agents all day long. An agent in production costs a few dozen euros a month in usage — far less than an hour of data entry a day. The details, with dated benchmarks, in How much does AI cost.

6. The pitfalls

  • AI makes mistakes: check the numbers before using them, especially the ones it read in a document.
  • Keep humans on the customer relationship — the AI prepares, it doesn't replace the contact or the advice.
  • A connector means access. Give the AI the permissions of the person talking to it, not the administrator's, and keep writes behind a validation. → Is my data safe with AI?
  • Don't over-automate what isn't broken, and don't launch a second agent before you've proven the first.
