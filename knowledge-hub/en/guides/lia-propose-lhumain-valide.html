AI proposes, humans validate:
the guardrails that hold

The more AI acts, the more the framework matters. I've broken a production deploy, let an agent burn its budget in a loop, and discovered a pricing overhaul "already in prod" while testing it. Each incident became a rule. Here are the guardrails I apply everywhere — from the smallest script to my internal systems — and why they set you free more than they hold you back.

In 30 seconds

  • Automatic reads, validated writes. Read, refresh, analyze, propose: automatic. Change a price, a stock level, a campaign, an email, a post: human validation, log, measurement.
  • Three regimes depending on the nature of the action: mechanical → automatic; factual → generated then validated with one click; judgment → human draft, always.
  • Four things before launching an agent: an explicit objective, a budget cap, a bounded frequency, a measurable success criterion. And the AI checks what it assumes before coding.

1. Three incidents, three rules

I'm not theorizing. Every rule below has an incident behind it:

  • May 2026 — the production deploy that broke everything. An entire branch merged into production, with mixed developments (mine and the integrator's). Several things broken. Hard rule, carved into my dev context file the next day: never a whole branch, always the specific commit, and a mandatory question before any deploy. → the entry.
  • The agent stuck in a loop. An agent that relaunched itself every day, ate its budget and produced nothing usable. Rule: objective, budget, frequency, success criterion — or no agent. → the agent guide.
  • August 2026 — the overhaul discovered while testing it. An overhaul of the per-variant pricing system was already in production, without formal confirmation; a discrepancy between Odoo and the site revealed it. Rule: test for real rather than trust the docs — and a system where the AI checks what it assumes before writing a line. → the entry.

It's Compound Engineering applied to safety: the incident doesn't just get fixed, it hardens the system so it can't happen again.

Three details that make the difference:

  • The preview before validation. You validate a change you can see ("these 300 prices are going from X to Y"), not an intention ("align the prices"). My price-alignment engine first ran for weeks in observation mode — it simulated — before I switched product families to live, one by one.
  • Idempotent execution. Running the same action twice must not produce two effects. That's what makes mistakes recoverable.
  • Measurement after the action. A recommendation without measurement doesn't close the loop: it isn't "learned." The result is fed back into the next recommendation.

4. The four things before launching an agent

  1. An explicit objective — "we'll know it's good when…". A weak criterion = guaranteed wasted time.
  2. A hard budget cap — in euros or in calls. My daily hi-fi watch is capped at a few euros a month and picks its model automatically: it can no longer break on an update or run away.
  3. A bounded frequency — no permanent loop. A recurring task of under three days is a loop that expires on its own; a permanent task is a real scheduled task, monitored.
  4. A measurable success criterion — and an alert when it isn't met (a source dead for three days, an expected import that never arrives).

5. Have it checked before coding

The most profitable guardrail of the year isn't in the deploy, it's before: at the moment of the request. Odoo change requests used to arrive as a single line — no where, no what-happens-on-click, no "what must not change." My internal pipeline now frames them: a guided form (eight fields, dictation possible), screenshots and a transcribed video, then a two-pass AI review — it asks structured questions, the server checks them in the real database in read-only mode, the AI concludes.

Result on the first cases: a development request requalified as ten minutes of configuration, and a "solution" the AI showed didn't remove the supplier calls it claimed to avoid. About a hundred seconds and a few cents per review. The lesson: the AI doesn't code my request, it checks what it assumes. → the entry.

6. The non-negotiable locks

  • The assistant never has more permissions than the person talking to it. A whitelisted login, roles, and no possible escalation from within a conversation.
  • No secrets in the code, nor in the Brain, nor in a context file. Keys live in server-side environment variables; a scan blocks any commit that would contain one.
  • No cron that publishes on its own. A publication is an explicit, idempotent action, with a readable error.
  • Hard-blocking embargo, restricted people excluded from social queues (minors included), LinkedIn as draft only, never a judgment under the magazine's byline — the Content Machine locks.
  • Surgical deploys. The specific commit, never the whole branch; a parallel session always restarts from the up-to-date version; in case of conflict, reapply only your own files.
  • No automated engagement on social networks. No likes, no follows: inbound is a reader relationship, not growth hacking.

7. Why guardrails set you free

Guardrails are often presented as a brake. It's the opposite. Because the pricing engine can't sell at a loss and ignores absurd offers, I can let it run on six product families without watching it. Because the invoice agent can only attach an invoice to an order that exists, I can let it read my emails every fifteen minutes. Because Growth changes nothing without validation, I can plug all my tools into it.

The framework isn't what keeps you from automating. It's what lets you automate more — while you sleep.

What about Claude Code's auto mode?

Since August 14, 2026, Claude Code runs a project's commands back to back without asking for permission at every step: that's auto mode, the default. It doesn't contradict the rule, it sharpens it. Auto mode covers a repository's commands — reading, modifying files, running a test, deploying this site — and a classifier reviews every action to block whatever goes beyond the request, touches unknown infrastructure, or looks dictated by poisoned content. My prohibitions written in the repository take precedence over it. What it doesn't touch: writes into my business tools. Changing a price, sending an email, publishing: always a preview and a button.

The number that convinced me, measured by Anthropic on a thousand testers: reviewing every request, humans intercepted 13.6% of dangerous commands; auto mode, 89%. And human vigilance dropped to 5% after fifty validations. Clicking "yes" fifty times protects nothing. The useful control is elsewhere: in the written prohibitions, the diff you re-read, the health script that passes — and the final button on anything that commits you.
