What is "context,"
and why it's everything

We all started out looking for the right prompt. The real lever is elsewhere: in everything the AI knows at the moment you talk to it — and in what it no longer knows once the conversation drags on. Context explained simply: the window, what goes in, what falls out, and the four reflexes that change the quality of everything.

By Hugo Lahutte · September 1, 2026 · ~6 min read

In 30 seconds

  • Context is everything the model "sees" at the instant it answers: your instructions, the context files, the attached documents, the conversation history, the results of its tools. Nothing else. It has no hidden memory.
  • It all fits in a limited window (up to a million tokens on Claude 5 models). The fuller it gets, the more attention gets diluted: a long conversation gets worse, not better.
  • The craft is called context engineering: organizing what the AI knows (CLAUDE.md, Brain, skills), splitting things up (one conversation per topic, subagents for heavy reads), and knowing when to close with a handoff brief.

1. The context window: the workbench

Picture a workbench. Everything laid out on it, the model can read and use to answer. Everything that isn't on it doesn't exist for the model — even if you told it yesterday, even if it's in another thread. That bench is the context window, and it's measured in tokens (fragments of words).

It's big — a million tokens on recent models, several books' worth — but two things make it less comfortable than it looks. First, everything laid out on it is re-read at every response: a long conversation costs more and answers more slowly. Second, the model's attention gets diluted: the more cluttered the bench, the more likely it is to miss the detail that matters, mix up two instructions, or reopen a decision you'd already settled three hours earlier. A conversation that drags on is not a conversation that gets better.

2. What goes into the context (by layers)

The right way to think about it is in layers, from the most stable to the most volatile:

  1. The rules — the CLAUDE.md. How to work with me. Changes little, loaded every time: hence the obsession with keeping it short.
  2. The knowledge — the Brain. What we know. Changes all the time, loaded on demand: the page for the entity at hand, not the whole wiki.
  3. The procedures — the skills. Loaded only when the request matches, so they don't pollute conversations that don't need them.
  4. The tools — the connectors. Every connected tool adds its description to the context; plugging in forty connectors "just in case" costs space at every response.
  5. The conversation — today's request, the attached documents, the history.

This hierarchy is the same as the one in my method. That's no coincidence: the method is a way of organizing context.

3. When it overflows: compaction, handoff brief, capture

When the bench is full, the tool compacts: it replaces the beginning of the conversation with a summary. It's automatic in Claude Code and in the API. It's useful, and it's risky: the summary keeps what's essential according to the model, not necessarily according to you. I've already seen a session pick back up after compaction having forgotten a constraint I'd set at the start — it was in the summarized part, and the summary had smoothed it over.

My answer is twofold, and it's in my CLAUDE.md:

  • The handoff brief. When the conversation drags on, Claude offers to close with a brief: where things stand, what's decided, what's left, the constraints. I start over in a fresh conversation with that brief. A clean bench, and nothing forgotten — because I'm the one who validated what had to stay.
  • The capture. Whatever durable thing the session produced (a decision, a lesson, a project status) doesn't stay in the history: it goes in the Brain. Tomorrow's context gets built today.

4. What about "memory"?

Assistants have memory features: Claude retains your preferences and the context of your projects from one conversation to the next — and since August 2026, that memory is shared between chat and Cowork. It's handy for what's implicit (your style, your ongoing projects). But it's a memory you never re-read, that's hard to correct, and that stays inside one tool.

The Brain is chosen memory: explicit, re-readable, versioned, correctable by hand, read by all my tools. The two coexist. The rule I apply: whatever commits a decision or describes a project goes in the Brain; whatever is about convenience ("he prefers short lists") can stay in the tool's memory.

5. The four reflexes

Reflex 1One conversation per topicNew topic, new conversation. Mixing the fall strategy and an Odoo bug in the same thread degrades both.
Reflex 2Load the right file, not everythingThe page for the entity at hand, the matching skill, the useful document. Not "the whole folder just in case."
Reflex 3Delegate heavy readsA long report, an entire repo: a subagent reads and returns the conclusion. The main conversation stays light.
Reflex 4Close with a handoff briefBefore it overflows: a brief validated by you, a fresh conversation, a capture in the Brain.

6. From the prompt to the system

Eighteen months ago, the fashionable skill was prompt engineering: finding the formula. It hasn't gone away — a good prompt still counts — but it has become the small part of the job. What separates someone who "uses ChatGPT" from someone who ships things to production with AI is what they've built around the conversation: stable rules, up-to-date knowledge, filed procedures, framed tools, and the discipline of clearing the bench.

The right mental model: you're not writing prompts, you're managing the attention of a very competent, very distractible collaborator who only knows what you've put in front of them.
