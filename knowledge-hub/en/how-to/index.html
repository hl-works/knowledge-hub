How I use Claude

In fifteen months, the way I work with Claude has changed in nature. At first, a file of instructions pasted into a chat. Today, a system: versioned files, a private brain, three ways of using Claude, two agents, packaged procedures and guardrails. Here's the setup as it actually runs — and where to start if you're just getting going.

By Hugo Lahutte · September 1, 2026 · First version: May 30, 2026 · ~12 min read

In 30 seconds

  • Context is everything, not the prompt. What makes Claude good in my setup is what it knows about me before I type the first line.
  • Two layers of context: the CLAUDE.md files (how to work with me) and the Brain (what we know: entities, projects, decisions, method), a private wiki in the open OKF format that all my Claude instances read.
  • Three doors: Chat writes and thinks, Cowork executes office work, Code acts inside my projects. Two agents (HL and Cobra), skills for procedures, connectors for tools.
  • The loop that makes it all grow: every error becomes a rule, every session becomes a capture. And one golden rule: the AI proposes, the human validates.

1. The principle: context is everything

When you're starting out, you think quality comes from the prompt — the right formula, the magic phrase. That's wrong, or rather it's 20% of the story. The other 80% is context: everything Claude knows before you write your first line. Who you are, what you do, how you like to be answered, what's already been decided, what's already broken.

The craft has had a name since 2025: context engineering. That's what my method is — organizing what Claude knows, in two layers that don't mix:

  • How to work with me → the CLAUDE.md files (section 2).
  • What we know → the Brain, my private wiki (section 3).

The first layer rarely changes (tone, rules, principles). The second changes all the time (projects, decisions, the state of ongoing work). Keeping them apart stops the rules from drowning in the facts, and the facts from hardening into rules.

Today's conversationThe request, the attached files, the tool results. Ephemeral.
The CLAUDE.md files — “how to work with me”Tone, format, principles, hard rules, extensions by domain. Stable, versioned.
The Brain — “what we know”Entities, building blocks, projects, method, notes, sessions. Living, dated, private.
Skills and connectorsThe procedures loaded on demand, and the tools plugged in (Shopify, Drive, GitHub…).

2. The CLAUDE.md files: “how to work with me”

The historical core of the system hasn't moved: .md files (Markdown, plain text) that Claude reads at startup. A main CLAUDE.md that sets my tone (informal, direct, no preamble), my four working principles (Karpathy-style), my behavior rules (honesty, web search on anything that may have changed, “I don't know” rather than making things up) and context management.

Then extensions, loaded depending on the domain:

  • CLAUDE-strat.md — strategy, marketing, communications, business.
  • CLAUDE-dev.md — the generic coding conventions (discipline, deliverables, framed autonomous loops).
  • CLAUDE-dev-cobra.md — the Cobra-specific rules (surgical Odoo deploys, Shopify sync, topics in progress).

What's changed since May: these files no longer live only in a chat's “Project Instructions.” They're versioned in the repositories (a docs/ folder, a CLAUDE-{domaine}-{projet}.md naming convention), read automatically by Claude Code, and re-read on a fixed schedule — because a wrong context file is worse than no file at all. I told the story of the refresh that exposed stale critical points (hosting, deploy workflow, number of modules): Claude was starting every session with a wrong mental model of the infrastructure.

Every brand also has its own DNA file: history, positioning, what we say and what we don't. An hour of writing with Claude, and everything it produces about the brand goes up a level.

3. The Brain: “what we know”

This is the real new thing of summer 2026. Until then, what I knew about my projects lived in Claude project documents, not shareable between tools, and in my head. Since August, all of it lives in a Brain: a private git repository of Markdown files with a small header (type, title, description, tags, date, confidentiality), organized in seven folders:

  • entities — the group's organizations (Cobra, Haute Fidélité, HiFi Lovers, HL Consulting…): what it is, where it lives online, my position, what runs on it;
  • building blocks — the systems under construction, with their living specifications and their dated decisions;
  • projects — anything with a beginning and an end;
  • method — how I work (prescriptive: a rule to follow);
  • notes — what I think (revisable: an opinion to cite, with its “what could take it down” section);
  • sessions — the raw log;
  • personal — always private.

The format is called OKF (Open Knowledge Format), an open specification published by Google Cloud in June 2026 that formalizes the “LLM wiki” popularized by Andrej Karpathy. Nothing to install: Markdown, links between files, an index at the root. My Claude instances read it through a plain GitHub connector, starting from the index and following the links. No vector database, no server — as long as navigation is enough. The day “find me what we said about order dedup” no longer surfaces the right document, that'll be the signal to build the index. Not before.

Two rules hold the Brain together:

  1. A stale brain is worse than an empty brain. A missing document triggers a search; a wrong document triggers a decision. Hence the capture ritual (section 7), which updates the reference pages and not just the log.
  2. We never invent the content of a shell (empty placeholder page). An empty doc that says it's empty is honest. A doc filled in by guesswork is a trap that goes off six months later, exactly when you trust it.

The Brain stays private. This Knowledge Hub is its curated version, rewritten to be read by humans and cited by AIs. Moving from one to the other is an editorial act, never a sync.

4. Chat, Cowork, Code: who does what

Three doors into the same Claude, and a split that structures all my work:

ChatWrite, think
In my setup
Angle, text, tone, strategy. It has my voice. We stop at Markdown: no HTML, no git.
Example
Writing this page, framing a spec, settling a positioning.
CoworkDelegate office work
In my setup
Files, spreadsheets, presentations, built-in browser, scheduled tasks — no terminal. On desktop, web and mobile since summer 2026.
Example
Preparing a deck, going through a file, running a Monday routine.
CodeAct inside projects
In my setup
Reads and edits files, runs commands, deploys, updates the internal linking. In the terminal, on the web or from my phone.
Example
An Odoo module, this page live a minute after the push, an internal app.

The boundary rule is simple and I stick to it: Chat writes, Code executes. A Chat session produces a validated Markdown; Code turns it into a deliverable (HTML page, JSON-LD, sitemap, llms.txt, commit, push). The two halves have different success criteria — the accuracy of the argument on one side, conformance to the template on the other. Mixing them in one session means losing both.

5. Two agents, two accounts

I run two Claude instances, deliberately kept apart:

  • Claude · HL — my personal account: steering, strategy, editorial, method, this site, the group's building blocks. Extension CLAUDE-strat.md.
  • Claude · Cobra — the company account: Odoo and Shopify development, purely technical. Extension CLAUDE-dev.md + Cobra. It knows the codebase by heart and never talks about my personal projects.

Both write in the same journal (the Claude · HL and Claude · Cobra badges on each entry) and read the same Brain — but the Cobra Claude only writes to the Cobra pages, the sessions and the technical building blocks. Not a technical permission: a governance rule, so as not to mix two perimeters in a base read from both sides.

6. Skills and connectors: the procedures and the arms

Two building blocks have taken up a lot of room this year.

Skills. A skill is a procedure packaged in a folder (a SKILL.md file, sometimes scripts) that Claude loads when the situation calls for it. In my setup, three do most of the work:

  • kh-post-brief — at the end of a session, with any AI, produce the brief for a journal entry: title, vertical, tags from the whitelist, sections, sanitization guardrails. I paste the brief into Claude Code, which publishes the FR + EN entry and all the internal linking.
  • brain — read, search and update the Brain, with a strict write gate: never a write without an explicit instruction.
  • wiki-capture — the end-of-session ritual (section 7).

A skill's strength: it's portable. The text of kh-post-brief works pasted into ChatGPT or Gemini. It's written method, not magic.

Connectors. Claude plugged into my tools through the MCP standard: Shopify (both stores), Google Drive, Gmail, GitHub, Qonto, the host of my apps, Odoo through code. Result: it works on my real tools, not on a demo — and it can now send an email or move a file, under validation. One non-negotiable rule: the assistant never has more rights than the person talking to it.

→ The guides: What is a skill (and a hook, a subagent) · What is MCP.

7. The loop: every error becomes a rule, every session a capture

The guiding principle is still Compound Engineering: when Claude trips over a recurring point, I don't grumble, I add the rule. The file improves with use, and all future work benefits. An Odoo production release that broke several things became a hard rule in CLAUDE-dev-cobra.md; an agent burning through its budget in a loop became a standard guardrail.

Since August, the loop has a second level. At the end of a session, the capture skill does four things, in this order: re-read the session and identify the entity and the topic; write the sessions/ entry in the OKF format; propose targeted changes to the reference pages affected (building blocks, entities); run the confidentiality filter and propose the commit. The third point is the one you're tempted to skip — so it's the one at the top of the procedure. Without it, the log grows while the pages go stale in silence.

The trigger isn't automatic, and that's deliberate: no mechanism fires reliably on “the conversation is getting long” across every client. Instead, a rule in the CLAUDE.md: when the conversation gets long, Claude offers to wrap up with a handoff brief — and to capture into the Brain. It works everywhere, including on mobile, with no infrastructure.

Do→ Spot the error or the lesson→ Rule in CLAUDE.md+ Capture in the Brain→ Next session, higher up every session

→ The guide: Compound Engineering.

8. The guardrails: the AI proposes, the human validates

The more Claude acts, the more the framework matters. The rules I apply everywhere, from the smallest script to my internal systems:

  • An agent has four things before it's launched: an explicit goal (“we'll know it's good when…”), a budget cap, a bounded frequency, a measurable success criterion.
  • Automatic reads, validated writes. Reading, refreshing, proposing: automatic. Changing a price, a stock level, a campaign, an email, a publication: human validation. The standard flow: request → analysis → preview of the change → validation → execution → log → measurement.
  • Three regimes depending on the nature of the action: mechanical = automatic; factual reply = generated then validated with one button; judgment = human draft, always.
  • Check before coding. My Odoo change-request pipeline has the AI review every request in two passes: it asks structured questions, the server checks them against the real database in read-only mode, the AI concludes. It has already requalified a development request into ten minutes of configuration.
  • No secrets in the code or in the Brain, surgical deploys (never a whole branch, always the exact commit), no cron that publishes on its own.

9. Where to start

Look at existing files for inspiration, but don't copy them — they're tuned to a context that isn't yours. My recommendation, in order:

  1. Create a Project on claude.ai and ask Claude to build your CLAUDE.md with the prompt below. One hour, and you stop re-explaining yourself.
  2. Write the DNA file for your company or brand. That's where communications and content go up a level.
  3. Open a minimal Brain: a folder with an index.md, one page per organization (“what it is, where it is, my position, what runs on it”) and one method page. Markdown, a private repository, a connector. The rest fills in session after session — and never invent a page you don't know how to fill in.
  4. Move to Code or Cowork when you want Claude to do, not just answer. And set the four guardrails before the first agent.

The prompt to copy and paste to generate your files:

Prompt — generate your CLAUDE.md and your starter BrainI want to build my context system for Claude: a CLAUDE.md file, a DNA file for my organization, and a minimal Brain. I'm attaching CLAUDE.md, CLAUDE-strat.md and CLAUDE-dev.md files as references. Read them to understand the structure, the expected level of detail and the overall logic — but don't copy them: they're tuned to another context, not mine. What I want to build: 1. A main CLAUDE.md that covers: - My tone and preferred format (formal or informal? prose or lists? how long should answers be?) - My professional context: what I do, my company, my industry, my role - The behavior rules to enforce (web search on anything that may have changed, honesty, no flattery, “I don't know” rather than making things up, push back if my idea is bad) - What I DON'T want (preambles, end-of-answer summaries, frameworks I didn't ask for, over-complication) - Context management: when the conversation gets long, offer to wrap up with a handoff brief - The logic for calling the extension files: this file must know when to load the others depending on the context 2. A [NameOfMyOrganization].md file that describes its DNA: - History and context - What we do, for whom, why - Positioning and values - Brand tone: what we say, what we don't say - This file will be loaded whenever Claude produces content related to this organization 3. A minimal Brain: an index.md and, for each important organization in my professional life, a Markdown page with a header (type, title, description, tags, timestamp, confidentialite) that answers four questions — what it is, where it lives online, what my position is, what systems run on it. If you don't know how to fill in a section, leave it empty and ask me: don't make anything up. 4. If I have recurring technical or business needs, we'll create the appropriate extensions (the equivalent of CLAUDE-dev.md or CLAUDE-strat.md) once the foundation is in place. Method: ask me ONE question at a time. We build section by section. When we've covered everything, you generate each complete file in Markdown, separately.

That's all you need to get started. The rest builds itself through use: every friction becomes a rule, every session becomes a capture, and your system gets better on its own.

10. What's changed since May 2026

For those who read the first version of this page — or who want to gauge how fast the ways of working are moving:

  • The CLAUDE.md files are versioned in the repositories and split by convention (generic / specific), instead of living in a chat.
  • The Brain exists (August 2026): knowledge has moved out of project documents to become a private wiki, readable by all my tools.
  • Cowork has become a real third door: desktop, web, mobile, built-in browser, memory unified with the chat, scheduled routines.
  • Skills have replaced reusable prompts. A procedure lives in a folder, not in a notepad.
  • Connectors act, not just read: sending an email, managing a file — under validation.
  • The Claude 5 family (Sonnet 5 in June, Opus 5 in July, Fable 5.1 on September 1) has changed the math: the right model is still the cheapest one that does the job, but “the job” has become much longer and more autonomous.
  • Guardrails have become systems, not instructions: validation before writing, log, measurement after action.
